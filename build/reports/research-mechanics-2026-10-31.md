# Which game mechanics teach which language: evidence for the trips brainstorm

Research note, 31 Oct 2026. It builds on `s12-research-cefr-and-book.md`, which covered CEFR levels, Duolingo, the book games, retrieval, spacing and the production effect. That ground isn't repeated here. Each source in Proof is marked **read** (I opened the page or the PDF) or **summary** (search-engine summary only, because the page was blocked or behind a paywall). Evidence strength in the table:

- **Strong:** a randomised trial or a meta-analysis.
- **Moderate:** a few controlled studies, or one good one.
- **Low:** small classroom studies, practice wisdom or vendor claims.

Nothing here is about Kutchi, and very little is about heritage learners.

## Six findings that should steer the brainstorm

1. **The language has to be the move itself, not a gate before the fun.** In *Zombie Division*, children aged 7–11 learned more when the maths was the attack itself. Asked as a quiz between levels, it taught less, and in free time they played the integrated version 7 times longer (Habgood and Ainsworth 2011; summary). Hirsh-Pasek's four pillars say the same in plain terms: a shooter that only needs swiping isn't "active" learning, and "side games unrelated to the learning goal" make children drift (read). So the test for any idea is whether the heard word decides what happens. If the word is a toll before the fun, drop the idea.
2. **Verbs come from doing and seeing movement.** In a randomised trial, 111 preschoolers learned 14 foreign words over 4 weeks. Acting the words out with the whole body gave the best recall, still there 6 weeks later. Gesture alone did less, and sitting and repeating did least (Mavilidi et al. 2015; summary; Toumpaniari et al. 2015, read). Children under 3 learned verbs from video only with a live person beside them. Children over 3 could learn some from video alone (Roseberry 2009; read). On a tablet, the nearest thing to acting is the child making a character act: an animated action, set off by the child, where the verb decides which action happens.
3. **Children speak when someone needs them to, and the recogniser decides what can be asked.**
   - An agent that asked 3–6 year olds questions during a story matched an adult for comprehension, but the children said less to it than to a person (Xu et al. 2022; read).
   - Children aged 8–9 who "taught" a robot kept more words than children who practised alone. Children who knew less gained most (2025; read).
   - In a voice game, preschoolers whose speech wasn't recognised kept trying: they repeated, got louder and changed their tone. They stopped only when a parent said the app was broken (Cheng et al. 2018; read).
   - Children in voice games shout, stretch words ("juuump"), stutter-start ("g-g-go") and talk over each other. Standard spotters failed on children even with two words (Disney Research, Mole Madness; read).
   - Our own numbers point the same way. With child-like pitch and tempo shifts, the general settings named 19 of 60 takes and the two-choice setting named 49 of 60, with no wrong answers (`js/shared/speech.js`, SH-127).

   So speaking moments should be slow, one turn at a time, with **2–3 options that sound different**, never against the clock.
4. **Speed and listening fight each other.** A Tetris vocabulary game for adults had to pause the blocks so players could recall and speak. Speaking made players clear fewer rows (Cai, MIT; read). Distracting game extras cost children more than adults, and cost more under time pressure (seductive-details research; summary). Studycat says it removed timers from its children's games (vendor; summary). Our window ride already follows the right rule: things are asked only as they come into view, and a missed thing comes round again.
5. **The same scene repeated teaches better; the same mechanic repeated gets boring.** Three-year-olds who heard one story three times kept new words well above chance a week later (59–72% correct, chance 25%). Those who heard three different stories with the same words scored at chance (Horst et al. 2011; read). Engagement with one game falls after the first weeks, and new content released over time held a small study's children better (poster; summary). So keep the scene and the words steady while they're being learned. Bring new mechanics for new *kinds* of language, not for novelty, and use new skins for the trips.
6. **Each type of word has its own trap.**
   - **Adjectives:** these mean "this one, not that one of the same kind". Four-year-olds use that cue and three-year-olds don't, and contrast ("it's not square") helps (summary). Colour words are learned slowly, over years (summary).
   - **Positions:** in and on are learned before under, and context steers the answer, so a cup goes "in" whatever is said (Bremner and Idowu; summary).
   - **Questions:** children understand and ask what, where and who before why, how and when (summary).
   - **Numbers:** "Give me three" is the standard test of whether a child knows what a number word means; most pass it around age 4 (summary).

## The big table

Leak-test fixes used below:

- **(a) The word picks:** every thing on screen could be right; only the heard word decides.
- **(b) Fewer asked than shown,** with learned words as decoys.
- **(c) Answer by sound or voice,** never by matching a picture.
- **(d) No winning by waiting or speed:** help comes only after a try, and nothing leaves before it's been asked.

"Speaking set" is how many options the recogniser checks.

## The 10 most promising for this game

Ranked for the language the child still needs (verbs, describing words, positions, questions, speaking), what our recogniser can do, and the leak test.

1. **Nani says, do it, with the verb deciding.** This is the cheapest strong route to verbs, with the best evidence (embodiment trials). The design point that matters: one thing, several actions. It fits any trip: animals, people at the beach, a kitchen pot.
2. **You be Nani: give the order.** This is the speaking flip of number 1, and the most natural way to make the child say verbs. It's a real two-person exchange (speaking.md's rule), the child has heard every command first, and 2–3 verbs is a set the recogniser can manage.
3. **Who's doing it?** Verbs heard against moving scenes, which suits children over 3 (Roseberry). It reuses Find it and Snap with animated people, so it's a skin, not a new engine.
4. **Call it over.** Two choices, a reason to speak, and an instant reward (the animal comes). It's the speaking moment most likely to work with real children's voices. It only builds names and a few nouns, so it's a starter rung, not a curriculum.
5. **The X one, with contrast pairs.** The cleanest leak-proof adjective game (all the same noun). It suits stalls, clothes, fruit and animals of different sizes. Expect colours to come slowly; teach size, hot or cold and full or empty first.
6. **Put it where, and Tell Ali where.** Positions both ways on an engine we already have. Make sure the place doesn't suggest the answer, so mix in, on and under for the same object. Bring in "under" after in and on.
7. **Behind the screen.** The one mechanic where positions and describing words make the child both listen and talk, with a natural reveal and no verdict: "look, the same!" or "oh, a bit different". It's a new engine, but a small one.
8. **Ask to find out.** The only mechanic that makes the child *ask*, which speaking.md calls the hardest part. A hidden thing plus a family member who knows gives the reason to ask; 2–3 heard questions (where, who, what) give the closed set. Hide and seek at home is the obvious first one.
9. **Shopkeeper, both ways.** Numbers, nouns and the ordering frame. Cook already proves it works; trips can reskin it as many stalls. It's also the natural home for "Give me N".
10. **Fix the muddled toy.** Low pressure: the child corrects someone who's sillier than them, and children who know less gain most. The child must say the right word, not just "no". It's untested under 8, so treat it as a trial.

Not ranked, but useful glue: **What's missing**, answered by voice (for recall on any topic), and **an action song** on the ride (for verbs and numbers). The song depends on Mum or Masi singing; it can be a clapping chant with no instruments if Zafar prefers that.

## Verdict: the four-choice quiz

- **For 3–5 year olds:** a poor fit. Four options is a heavy load (the advice for young children's apps is to limit options), a quarter of answers are guesses, and the show's drama is about risk and losing, which goes against non-negotiable 7.
- **For 6–8 year olds, as one stall at a fair:** fine if it changes shape.
  - Three choices, heard rather than shown (speakers, as in Name it), with learned words as decoys.
  - No 50:50, because that lifeline is elimination itself.
  - "Phone a friend" becomes **calling Nani**: she says the question again as a fuller sentence with a gesture. It's a hint that costs nothing.
  - A ladder that only goes up; a wrong answer just means "try another".
  - The prize is a sticker, a photo or something for Ali.
- **Honestly,** it's Choose the reply and Name it in a costume, so it adds occasion, not new teaching. Build it as a skin on existing engines, not as a mode.

## Verdict: an arcade trip

**Worth it as a fair day, if every machine is an engine we already trust in a new skin, and the rules stay strict.**

- **The heard word decides every move** (intrinsic integration).
- **Slow at the moment of listening:** nothing leaves before it's been asked, and there are no timers. Speed can rise between words, never during one.
- **No chance at all:** the crane never misses, and the pusher drops exactly the number pushed.
- **No guns:** use water squirters, hoopla or beanbags.
- **Prizes:** stickers, a photo, a toy for Ali; never sweets, never "credits".
- **No casino-style lights or sounds.** The research on childhood coin pushers and crane machines (Newall 2021, replicated 2023) links them, by recall, to adult gambling, partly through "near-wins". So no near-misses either.
- **Listening only.** Fast voice play is where children's speech recognition fails, so speaking stays at the calm stalls, such as ordering a snack at the fair.

## Flaws and limits

- **The evidence is thin where we need it most.** The best trials are about nouns, adults or school-age children, in English or Italian. Nothing tests these mechanics for a heritage language, for Kutchi, or for children who can't read. Most of the game research (TPR, Kim's game, I-spy, barrier games, information gap) is small classroom action research.
- **No study puts a millionaire quiz, whack-a-mole or a claw machine in front of under-8s.** The verdicts reason from cognitive-load and integration studies, not from direct tests.
- **No independent study of any commercial kids' app was found.**
  - Studycat says VoicePlay runs on the device, is trained on children's voices and accepts "close enough"; Buddy.ai says it was trained on 5,000 hours of children's speech; Pili Pop and Dinolingo say they have speaking modes. All vendor claims.
  - Lingokids, as Common Sense Media describes it, has no speech recognition: it leans on videos, songs, mazes and memory games, and "it's not always clear what kids are supposed to do".
  - Gus on the Go uses tap-to-hear lessons, each ending in a review game. Mondly Kids uses flash cards.
  - Duolingo has no children's language app; Duolingo ABC teaches reading.
  - Heritage apps exist (a Punjabi app; MIRRI, where elders add their own voices; Stoyee's grandparent avatars), but none has been reviewed.
  - In short, the apps lean on tap-to-hear, picture choice, matching and songs, and almost none make a child speak in a two-person exchange. That gap is ours to fill.
- **Our recogniser has never been measured on a real child.** Every speaking row above assumes 2–3 options that sound different; that comes from SH-127 and the Disney work, not from children playing this game.
- **Blocked pages:** I couldn't open the Bell Foundation, the Studycat blog and VoicePlay pages, or the Victorian barrier-games sheet (403 or 503). Those points are search summaries.
- **Songs** need family members to sing, and whether instrumental music is wanted is a question for Zafar.
