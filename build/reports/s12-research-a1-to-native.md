# From A1 to native: what each level needs, and what the game has

Research note for Sprint 12, 23 Oct 2026. Builds on `s12-research-cefr-and-book.md` (which reached Pre-A1 to A1 and ranked the next book games); it does not repeat that. Sources are listed in Proof, marked read (page opened) or summary (search result only). Zafar's question: "what is needed vs what do we already have to reach the higher language levels, from A1 to fluent native."

**Headline.** The game alone can reach solid A1 listening and A1 speaking, and with the additions below A2 listening and low-A2 speaking. Nothing above that comes from a screen: B1 and up need a person who answers back, and native-like Kutchi needs a home that speaks it. The game's best role above A1 is to feed the family: keep words alive, supply the stories, and hand the grown-ups the lines.

## 1. The levels, in plain words

Reading and writing are left out: Kutchi has no standard script (Gujarati script in India, Perso-Arabic in Pakistan, Khojki historically; Wikipedia, read). The CEFR reading and writing scales do not apply, and rule 3 keeps written Kutchi away from the child anyway. The one literacy job is internal: one house spelling, confirmed by Zafar, so the lexicon stays consistent.

Hours are Cambridge's cumulative guided learning hours for adult English learners (Cambridge guide, read). FSI puts Hindi, Urdu and Gujarati in Category III, about 44 weeks or 1,012 class hours to professional working proficiency (state.gov, read); Kutchi would sit there. Vocabulary is Milton's English lemma count per level (Milton and Alexiou 2010, read) and Nation's word-family suggestion (Nation, read). All are adult figures.

| Level | Listening | Speaking: production and interaction | Adult hours (cumulative) | Vocabulary (Milton / Nation) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pre-A1 | Recognises familiar words said slowly in a known scene. | Single words, a few fixed phrases. | about 50 | a few hundred / about 120 survival words and phrases |
| A1 | Follows very slow, careful speech with pauses; understands a simple description of a room or an object; follows a short illustrated story heard several times (young-learner samples, read). | Asks and answers simple questions about needs and very familiar things; depends on repetition and the other person's help. | 90–100 | under 1,500 / "basic repertoire" |
| A2 | Understands sentences and common expressions about family, shopping, food, the day. | Simple routine exchanges; describes self, home, things in simple terms; the first past and future. | 180–200 | 1,500–2,500 / the most frequent 1,000 families |
| B1 | Main points of clear standard talk on familiar matters, school and play. | Connected talk; tells what happened, hopes, plans; gives brief reasons; copes with most everyday situations. | 350–400 | 2,750–3,250 / 2,000–3,000 |
| B2 | Main ideas of complex talk, concrete and abstract. | Fluent, spontaneous exchange with a native speaker without strain for either; argues a view. | 500–600 | 3,250–3,750 / about 4,000 |
| C1 | A wide range of long, demanding speech; implicit meaning. | Flexible, effective use for social, school and work purposes. | 700–800 | 3,750–4,500 / 5,000–6,000 |
| C2 | Virtually everything heard. | Precise, fluent, fine shades of meaning. | 1,000–1,200 | 4,500–5,000 / 7,000–9,000 |
| Native-like child | Everything a Kutchi-speaking 8-year-old hears at home and at a wedding. | Talks like one: fast, idiomatic, with the family's own forms and jokes. | Thousands of hours of real talk from infancy. | about 600 root-word meanings a year from infancy (Biemiller, read); about 6,000 by the end of year 2 of school (summary) |

Two CEFR details matter here. The 2020 Companion Volume adds mediation (passing on and explaining what someone said, between people or between languages); its scales start at A1 or A2 and are optional for young learners (Cambridge mediation paper and Pearson, summary; the Council of Europe PDF itself refused the download). For a heritage child mediation is the natural skill: telling Nani what Mum said, in Kutchi. And the Companion Volume states no native-speaker standard (Wikipedia, read): C2 is not "native"; "native-like" for a heritage child is a social fact, not a level.

Native-like is a question of input volume, not lessons. Hart and Risley's recordings give 620–2,150 adult words per hour and 13–45 million words heard by age 4 (summary). Grammar-learning ability stays high until about 17 (Hartshorne, Tenenbaum and Pinker 2018; summary), so the window is open through childhood, but heritage speakers' outcomes track quantity of input, use and age of onset (Montrul; Polinsky; summary). In De Houwer's survey of 1,450 Flemish families, children spoke both languages in 93% of homes where the parents spoke the minority language to each other, and in 36% where they did not (via Pearson 2010, read). English-dominant bilingual children in Unsworth's Dutch studies typically had under about 35% of their exposure in Dutch (summary).

### What gets people there

- **Comprehensible input and lots of it.** Starting age matters less than the amount of exposure; an early start without more hours gives little (Muñoz, summary). Extensive viewing of series over a school year raised children's and adolescents' vocabulary, more with repeated characters and pre-taught words (Pujadas and Muñoz 2019; summary). Children's films need about 7,000 word families for 98% coverage, but 95% coverage of everyday talk needs 2,000–3,000 (Nation 2006; van Zeeland and Schmitt 2012; summary).
- **Interaction with feedback.** Corrective feedback inside conversation has a medium-to-large effect (Mackey and Goo 2007: d = 0.71 immediate, 1.09 delayed; summary). In 15 classroom studies, feedback had durable effects, prompts beat recasts, and younger learners gained more (Lyster and Saito 2010; summary). This is the thing a game cannot do well: a real listener who misunderstands and repairs.
- **Output.** Saying it builds recall (the earlier report). Past A1, output means whole sentences, which needs open speech recognition that does not exist for Kutchi, or a human.
- **Explicit grammar.** Explicit beats implicit instruction in adult meta-analyses (Norris and Ortega 2000; Goo et al. 2015; summary), but the evidence is adult and the game's rule stands: patterns from many varied lines, a note for grown-ups only.
- **Spaced retrieval and stories:** covered in the earlier report; both scale to A2.

## 2. Per level: what the game has, what it would need

What the game has today: Cook (pantry and six stations), the clinic (five stages, twelve heal games), Find it, Conversations (module built, not wired), the evening book (Story by the Fire), First launch, the window ride, and the shared engines Count to a heard number, Pack the bag and Put it in its place; Dress up, Monsoon rush, Snap, Tidy up and Who did it are parked. The engine holds 964 entries (336 confirmed, 162 draft, 466 to record), 56 verbs, 330 nouns, 40 describing words, 26 postpositions, 2 conjunctions, 113 rules (38 confirmed, 63 unknown with Mum's questions attached), 417 family recordings and 593 clip rows. Known grammar: want/need agreement, gender and plural, one/two, lists (*ne*, *waari*), negation, this/that, where-phrases, question words, the respectful plural, a few "finished" forms, the "having done" form. The closed-set recogniser `Speech.listen` exists. Word cards fade over days; spaced words are planned.

| Level | Have | Need: mechanics | Need: content and voice | Need: engine | A game cannot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A1** | Picture-and-voice tasks that match Cambridge Starters; whole sentences built by the engine; 336 confirmed words; closed-set speaking in the eye test and kitchen | Say it; Choose the reply; Nani says, do it; spaced words (the earlier report's list) | 300–500 words heard 6–10 times each; about 40 question frames and the answers. Roughly 600–800 clips; we are at 417, so one or two more rounds with Mum | Settle the 63 unknown rules; questions and yes/no answers; possession (*jo/ji/ja*); imperatives; present "I want / I have / I like" | Nothing essential; A1 is in reach |
| **A2** | Our day (sequence); the conversations module (reply pills); the book shell; frequency-driven whole-line recording designed but not run | A talking day: retell today in the past, plan tomorrow; describe (I-spy); 3–4 turn dialogues; a growing story library heard many times | 1,000–1,500 words (about 700 new nouns, verbs and describing words); tense forms for 56+ verbs (6–12 cells each, 400–700 clips) or 300–500 recorded whole sentences from play-through frequencies; 30–50 two-minute stories. Roughly 2,000–3,000 clips: 15–25 hours of Mum's recording, so Masi and Zafar must share it | Past (with the object agreement Sindhi-type past takes), future, progressive; oblique case before postpositions; *because*, *and then*, *but*, *when*; comparatives; the question system | Speaking at A2 is routine exchange with a person; the game can rehearse it, not hold it |
| **B1** | The engine's shape (GF-style abstract syntax) can grow this far; nothing else | Open retelling judged on key words; story mode with choices; mediation tasks (tell Nani what Mum said) | 2,500–3,500 words; hundreds of stories; several voices. Beyond one family voice | Subordinate clauses (if, when, that), relative clauses, modals (should, can, must), reported speech, aspect | Fluency comes from talk; a Kutchi child-speech recogniser would need thousands of consented recordings that do not exist |
| **B2–C2** | Nothing, rightly | Not a game's job | 4,000–9,000 words; media and community Kutchi barely exist | Full grammar | All of it: school-level talk, idiom, register, argument |
| **Native-like** | The family, if it chooses | The game as prompt and ally for the household | Every day, in the house, from everyone | n/a | Be the home |

Time check. Fifteen minutes a day is about 90 hours a year, and games teach less per hour than lessons. On Cambridge's figures that is a year to A1 and two to three to A2 on the game alone; B1 (350–400 adult hours) is four years of daily play with no partner, which no child will do. That sets the ceiling.

## 3. What works for children elsewhere

- **Language nests.** Kōhanga Reo, from 1982, immersed infants in Māori when under 20% of Māori spoke it natively (Wikipedia, read). In the 111-child longitudinal study, children with more and better Māori at kōhanga, school and home scored higher (NZCER, read); ERO's 2018 review of 11 kōhanga found no benchmark evidence base (summary). Pūnana Leo (1984, 11 preschools in 2022) was the first indigenous immersion preschool in the US; its first graduates are fluent and raising children in Hawaiian (Wikipedia, read; leaders' testimony, summary). Lesson: daily immersion from infancy, with fluent elders in the room, run by families.
- **Welsh and Irish immersion.** Most learners acquire Welsh through early immersion (Estyn, summary), yet 79% of fluent Welsh speakers had a fluent parent and only 9% came from homes with no Welsh (Welsh Language Use Survey, read). In Gwynedd, 29% of pupils from non-Welsh homes missed the expected level against 10% from Welsh homes; 14% versus 37% reached the higher one (IWA, read). Irish-medium pupils reach competent Irish with persistent errors (Ó Duibhir, summary). Lesson: school alone gives B1–B2 with a learner's accent; the home makes the difference.
- **Master-Apprentice.** One fluent elder, one learner, no English, everyday activities; 10–20 hours a week works best, 5 is the minimum (Hinton's programme, slides read); no formal evaluation found. Lesson: this is Nani's house in real life; the game books the sessions.
- **Family language policy.** Minority language at home and parents speaking it to each other predicts active bilingual children (De Houwer, read via Pearson); exposure share matters (Unsworth, summary). Heritage children stay balanced in understanding but go English-dominant in speech by about 10 (earlier report).
- **UK complementary schools.** 153 London children aged 4–9: attenders self-reported higher heritage-language skill, mostly literacy (summary).
- **Digital tools for small and unwritten languages.** Living Dictionaries: free audio dictionaries, 400+ languages, 209,000 entries (summary). FirstVoices: community audio feeding dictionary apps; no outcome study found (summary). Cassels and Farr reviewed 32 Indigenous-language apps and call the evidence base small (abstract read). The one trial: a Quechua app with a teacher guide in Peruvian schools raised skills by 0.137 SD overall and 0.330 SD on app-aligned content, and raised Quechua use in public, most for the weakest starters (IDB 2026, abstract read). Duolingo's Irish, Hawaiian and Navajo courses report reach, not proficiency (summary). For Kutchi: uTalk lists 2,500 words and phrases; FreeLang a 500-word dictionary (summary). Lesson: apps shift words and willingness to speak, modestly, and work best wired to a teacher or a family.

## 4. Ceiling and roadmap

**Game alone:** solid A1 listening and speaking; with the additions below, A2 listening and low-A2 speaking (fixed exchanges and closed-set answers). **Game plus family:** A2–B1 speaking by 8–10 if the household holds a daily Kutchi slot the game feeds, and B1 listening from stories heard again and again. **Native-like:** only the home and the community, with the game keeping the words warm and giving the child something to say.

Ranked additions (after the earlier report's four: spaced words, Say it, What's missing, Choose the reply):

1. **The talking day: past and future** (unlocks A2 listening and the first A2 speaking). Our day retold in the past, tomorrow planned; the engine's abstract syntax gains tense, the paradigms gain cells. Effort: high (engine plus book, two builders, about a week). Recording: one paradigm round with Mum (about 60 verbs × core cells, 400–700 clips, or 300 whole sentences chosen by frequency); 10–15 hours of her time over several sittings.
2. **The story library** (A2 listening, vocabulary to 1,000+). Thirty to fifty two-minute stories built by the engine from the day's scenes, the top 200 lines recorded whole, repeated characters, the child filling gaps and later choosing turns. Effort: medium build; high content (a writing plan Mum confirms). Recording: 800–1,500 clips including about 700 new words, 10–20 hours, shared with Masi and Zafar.
3. **Family mode** (speaking past A1; the only route to B1). The grown-up gets one line to say and one question to ask at the table or on the weekly Nani call, in the engine's Kutchi with a replay; the child's answers are logged by a tap; word packs shared with cousins. Effort: low to medium (two screens, no new art). Recording: none beyond the existing clips. Also the only part that reaches the heritage research: it moves Kutchi into the house.
4. **Describe and deduce** (A2 descriptions): colours, sizes, positions, owners across Put it in its place, Pack the bag and Who did it, which already carry the leak rule for this. Effort: medium. Recording: about 150 clips (adjectives, number and position forms).
5. **Open speaking** (B1 speaking). Retell judged on key words with an extended family-template recogniser. Effort: very high; the models do not exist. Park it, but start collecting consented family and child recordings now so it is possible in two years.

Not recommended: a written Kutchi track, grammar notes for children, or B2+ content.
