# Nani jo Ghar — Questions for Mum (Combined, for the visit)

25 Sept 2026 · replaces Round 1 (grammar and first word list) and Round 2 (cooking). Nothing from those two is lost; the duplicates are gone and the order has changed so the most useful things come first.

*This is for a family game that teaches the children Kutchi, your Kutchi, the way it's spoken at home. Everything below is in English. It looks long: it isn't meant to be done in one go. Section A and Section B are the heart of it (about 20 minutes). After that, carry on for as long as you like and stop whenever you like.*

## How to answer

- **Record one long voice file** on the phone, in a quiet room. One sitting or several, it doesn't matter. Zafar will type what you say next to each question afterwards and send both back to you to check.
- **Before each section, say its letter out loud** ("Section A", "Section B"…), and before a numbered question say its number ("A3"). That's how the recording gets lined up with this sheet.
- **For a word list:** say the English, pause, say the Kutchi **twice**, then leave a gap of about two seconds. For example: *"red … laal … laal …"*. The gaps let each word be cut out on its own.
- **For a question:** answer in short, natural sentences, the way you'd actually say it to a grandchild in the kitchen. Not the textbook version, not the Gujarati or Hindi version: what our family says.
- **Not sure of a word? Say "skip"** and move on. **More than one way to say it? Say them all.** **If the family just uses the English word** (chips, T-shirt, plaster), say so: that's a real answer, not a gap.
- The two right-hand columns in every table are for Zafar to fill in afterwards. You don't need to write anything.

**Rough times:** Section A about 12 minutes · B about 8 · C about 6 · D about 15 · E about 15 · F about 15 · G about 8 · H about 20–30 (that one's a chat, not a list).

# The core: if you only have 20 minutes, do A and B

## Section A · How the language fits together (about 12 minutes)

*Why first: every word in the game gets stored in a way that depends on these answers. Learning them now saves redoing hundreds of words later. Just say the little phrases aloud; don't worry about explaining the rule unless you feel like it.*

### A1. Big, small, red: does the describing word change?

Please say each of these out loud, naturally. We'll listen for whether "big" and "red" change shape depending on what they describe (for example, whether it's *vadho* for some things and *vadhi* for others).

- a big cup · two big cups
- a big door · two big doors
- a big mango · a big kurta · a big dupatta
- a big maani · two big maani
- a small chair · a small bowl
- a red kurta · a red dupatta · a red car
- he is tall · she is tall · they are tall
- the big one · the small one · the red one · not the red one (pointing at things, no noun)

Then, if it's easy to say: is every object a "he" word or a "she" word? A few to sort if so: cup, door, chair, mango, shirt, maani, chai, plate, bowl, dupatta.

Answer notes:

### A2. One and more than one

- one cup · two cups · lots of cups
- one mango · three mangoes
- one maani · two maani · four maani
- one goat · two goats
- one eye · two eyes · one knee · two knees
- one spoon of sugar · three spoons of sugar (in the game we say *trae khun*: would you?)

Does the word for the thing itself change, or only the number in front of it?

Answer notes:

### A3. Asking for something, "and", "and then", "with", "for"

The game asks for everything with **"Muke {thing} khape"** (meant as "I need {thing}"), and adds extras with **"Ne {thing}"** ("and …") and **"Ne poi {thing}"** ("and then …").

1. At home, is *Muke chai khape* how someone would ask for chai? Or *Muke chai dine*, or *chai dhe*, or something else? Would a **child** say it one way to you, and **you** another way to a child?
2. "I need two maani": does anything in the sentence change for more than one thing, or for a "he" word versus a "she" word?
3. "Chai **with** milk" and "chai **with** two sugars": how do you tack the extras on? Is *ne dudh* ("and milk") natural, or is there a "with" word?
4. "Daal, **and then** the maani" (one after the other): is *ne poi* right?
5. "This one is **for** Nana" · "make one **for** Ma too".
6. "Give me the spoon" · "pass me the spoon" · "add the onion" · "take it".

Answer notes:

### A4. No, not, without, don't

Zafar has been using ***nar*** for all of these. Please say each one, as you'd really say it:

1. "Chai, **no** milk" · "**no** sugar" · "daal **without** onion" (leaving something out of an order)
2. "**Not** this one, that one" · "**not** the red one" (choosing)
3. "**Don't** touch that!" · "**don't** add the onion" · "**don't** run" (telling a child)
4. "No." (a plain answer to "do you want chai?") · "nothing" · "none left"

Is it the same word in all four, or different words?

Answer notes:

### A5. Where things are

Say each of these with any object you like, naturally:

- the cup is **on** the table · **under** the table · **in** the cupboard
- the key is **behind** the door · **next to** the plate · **in front of** Nana
- the spoon is **between** the two cups · **on top of** the shelf

Two things we'll listen for: does the word for "table" change when a position word follows it (the way Gujarati has *table-**ni** niche*)? Say "the table" on its own, then "under the table", and we'll hear it. And **left / right**: does the family say left and right at home, or "this side / that side"? Say "the left hand", "on the right", "in the middle", "in the corner".

Answer notes:

### A6. Things that happened

Please say these as you'd tell a grandchild:

1. "I went to the market"
2. "She gave me the mangoes"
3. "It stopped raining"
4. "Who ate the sweets?" · "Simba took it" · "I saw Simba in the kitchen"
5. "Where was it?" · "It was on the table"

(The first three are chosen because "I went" and "she gave" may work differently in Kutchi. Hearing them said naturally settles it.)

Answer notes:

What do you call a grandchild when you're talking to them (*beta*, *dikra*, a pet name)? Is there a different "you" for elders, and does it change the verb?

### H36. Which dishes are really ours?

These are the dishes we'd like in the game, a mix of Kutch cooking and the East African food we grew up with. For each: do you really make it, what's it called at home, and what would you add, remove or change? Which are more Kenya or Tanzania, and which are more Kutch? And what's missing that you'd expect in your kitchen?

Chai · maani / rotli · daal · chaat bowl (chana bateta) · samosa · mishkaki with chips · chips mayai · mogo · makai (corn on the cob) · mandazi · dahi puri / sev puri · dabeli · falooda · jalebi · sheer khurma · biryani / pilau

For the ones you really make, name five to ten things that go in each, and say if it belongs to an occasion (Eid morning, everyday dinner, guests, a rainy day).

Answer notes:

### H37. How you make the first six

For each, talk through what goes in and in what order, what you do at each step, and how you know it's ready. This becomes the recipe in the game.

1. **Chai:** water, tea, boil, milk, sugar? Masala, elchi, ginger? When do they go in?
2. **Maani / rotli:** flour, water, oil or ghee? How thin? When do you flip it? Ghee on top?
3. **Daal:** which daal? What goes in the tadka and in what order (jeeru, rai, hing, garlic, chilli…)? Onion, tomato?
4. **Chaat bowl:** every topping, in the usual order.
5. **Samosa:** which filling? How do you fold it?
6. **Mishkaki:** which meat, what else goes on the skewer, the marinade, the chips on the side with chilli and lemon?

Answer notes:

### H38. Who likes what

In the game everyone has their own taste and the child has to listen. Tell us the real ones, silly ones included: who takes three sugars, who has no milk, who won't touch chilli, who always wants extra onion, who hates what.
