# Review of the draft "chai's level" standard

For Zafar, 17 Nov. An independent read of `chai-standard.md`, the brief, chai's code and the old standard folder.

## Short answer

You are right. The draft is the old checklist again, smaller and better sorted. It should not be the standard. Three small parts are worth keeping; the rest has a one-time use.

## Why it feels wrong

1. **It is at the wrong level.** "Nothing makes a child feel bad" and "tap targets at least 48 px" sit side by side as equal lines. When everything is equal, a list gets ticked, not understood.
2. **Most of it describes what you can already see.** About 85 of the 105 lines say what chai does today. The brief's own proof for the rebuild is "old and new chai side by side: Zafar cannot tell them apart". A page that repeats chai is a copy, and the copy drifts.
3. **After the rebuild, the engine makes most lines true by itself.** The shelf draws its own shadows and pills; the engine owns the heat rule, the lights and the order. A maani builder cannot break them.
4. **It contradicts the brief.** The brief gives the judge "no checklist"; the draft opens with "the judge judges against it".
5. **A list did not catch the shadows last time.** The old standard ran to 31,000 words with a proof score per rule, and games still shipped flat. What catches a missing shadow is a look at the game beside chai, and a tray that draws its own shadow.

## Where I disagree with you, a little

"The code should explain it" is true only of the rebuilt code. Today's chai is one file of 2,461 lines of patches, and code never says *why*, nor what you want that is not built yet. Some of chai's behaviour also cannot be seen in one play: a light only the first time, "no" items from level 2, help on the third wrong try. For that, the draft is a good index of your feedback. Do not bin it. Demote it.

## What should exist instead

1. **Chai itself, rebuilt, is the standard.** Playable, with its numbers in one settings file.
2. **One page of principles** (draft below): the "why", as in your order-card example. It is for the next game's new situations, which chai cannot answer.
3. **A short "not in chai yet" list** on chai's game sheet: the two scenes' targets and the questions that block them. Each line is deleted when you sign it off, so the list shrinks to nothing.
4. **Each game's sheet says only where it differs from chai,** and why.
5. **The 105-line draft becomes the lead builder's moving inventory** for milestone 1: used once, so nothing of chai is lost as it moves into the engine. It is not carried forward as `standard-full.md`.

This changes the brief's section 6 and decision 3 (no "top-20 plus 105" pair) and step 5 of the check script.

## Draft: the principles behind chai

1. **Said before shown.** Nothing of an order appears until a family voice says it, and each card arrives as its phrase ends, because the child is here to listen and the picture should confirm the word, not replace it.
2. **The order first, the steps after.** The whole order is given, each item popping up big, before any instruction, because the child should know what they are making before being told how.
3. **One of everything.** One word card, one pop-up, one light, one kitchen, the same in every game, because a child learns the house once and a fix is made once.
4. **Show once, then trust.** An action is lit the first time only; after that Nani's voice alone, with help only when the child is stuck or wrong, because a light that stays is the answer and the Kutchi stops mattering.
5. **The thing itself tells you.** Time, heat, amounts and mistakes show in the object (a ring, bubbles, colour, a spoiled pan), never on a separate timer or as a verdict, because the child's eyes stay on the food.
6. **The child is never held up.** A tap lands at once, a tap skips speech, the next game loads behind this one, because waiting is where a young child leaves.
7. **The game moves on by itself.** No tick or next button; a step ends when the last thing asked is done, because buttons are reading and housekeeping, not play.
8. **Not before it is ready.** Nothing can be taken or turned before the green, in any game, because the timing is the skill and it must mean the same everywhere.
9. **A mistake costs little and shames nobody.** Things can be taken back, only the one dish is redone, and there is no cross or buzz in play, because a child who feels judged stops trying.
10. **Every dish belongs to a person.** It carries their face, goes to them, is what they asked for, and is thanked, because the game is about feeding family, and that is why the words matter.
11. **One thing at a time.** One voice, one light, and a reward that finishes before anything new asks for the eye, because a child follows one thing.
12. **Heard and seen together.** A sound starts with its picture and every action has both, because that pairing is how the word sticks.
13. **Everything is real and sits somewhere.** Real pictures, one light from the upper left, a soft shadow, nothing empty, floating or cut off, because one flat or empty thing makes the whole kitchen look unfinished.
14. **Everything glides.** Nothing jumps, snaps, re-zooms or changes style in front of the child, because a jump reads as a fault and breaks the feeling of a real place.

The test for adding a fifteenth: would it settle a question in a game not yet designed? If it only describes chai, it belongs in chai.

## Draft: not in chai yet

**Targets, in your words** (the draft's two "Target" blocks, kept as they are):
- The order: each card flies in as its own phrase ends; one tall kitchen painting, framed higher, people larger, plain wall behind, nothing on the island.
- The serve: everyone where they ordered from; one walnut tray per person, dish large and centred; no empty trays; a thank-you at every serve.

**To decide now (8 of the 18):**
1. Does the dish's card fly into the order card at every level?
2. Thank-you: at each serve, at the end, or both?
3. One thank-you per person, or per dish?
4. "Thank you" in English, or a Kutchi line for Mum to record?
5. With no guest, does Nani say thank you?
6. Do serving trays cast a shadow now?
7. Shadow strength: half the mock's, or stronger?
8. The end screen: rebuild today's two pages, or wait for your redesign?

The other ten are not chai questions. They go to the sheet of the game that raises them (maani, corn, sekelo), to the brief's own decisions (art, round length), or to your first play of rebuilt chai (pace with several pans; the redo at level 1).

## How it is used without becoming a checklist

- **The builder** reads the principles once. A new game is built from its sheet; where the sheet is silent, it does what chai does; where chai has no answer, the principles decide.
- **The judge** gets no page to tick. It plays the game beside chai and lists the ten most obvious differences, worst first, using the principles only to say why one matters. A planted fault proves it is looking.
- **You** read one page, once, and correct it by voice. After that your play is the proof.

## Verdict

1. You are right: 105 rules is the checklist again; do not make it the standard.
2. Keep three small things: one page of principles, the two scenes' targets, eight questions.
3. Chai rebuilt is the standard; the draft is a one-time inventory for the rebuild, then left behind.
