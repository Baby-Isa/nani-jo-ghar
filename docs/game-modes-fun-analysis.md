# Nani jo Ghar: what's actually fun (personas, self-critique, how hit games work)

**Date:** 23 Sept 2026
**Why:** Zafar's concern. The kitchen rush feels like a real, standalone game; the other modes don't. Sharing engines risks everything feeling the same (is the stall rush just the kitchen rush in a different room?). Before building more, understand in depth how popular games actually work and what makes them fun, then choose a small set of modes that are *each* genuinely good.
**Status:** analysis and recommendation. Section 7 proposes what to build first.

**What the personas tell us:**
- **Primary target:** ages **6–11 plus the parent sitting with them**.
- **A 5-year-old** needs a no-timer "relaxed" setting and voice-first cues.
- **Adults** need a short daily hook and a mode with depth.
- **No single mode satisfies everyone,** so the lineup needs one *toy-like, forgiving* mode, one *skill* mode with a high ceiling, and a *meta* layer (decorate and upgrade) that ties them together.

## 2. Self-critique of my earlier proposals

| What I said | What's wrong with it |
|---|---|
| "8 modes on 4 shared engines" | **Padding.** Several were chosen to fill the Roadmap's 8 slots, not because they're fun. Zafar is right that the stall rush is the kitchen rush in a different room. |
| "Proven genre = fun" | A *thin* clone of a hit isn't fun. The fun in those games comes from **tuning and "juice"** (feel, feedback, pacing), which takes iteration. The genre is a starting point, not a guarantee. |
| Sharing engines | Sharing *plumbing* (queues, timers, scoring, audio) is fine. Sharing the **core verb** (what your hands do) is what makes games feel the same. |
| Time pressure everywhere | Understanding speech needs thinking time, and timers reward guessing. Timers must be optional or gentle, and scale with how well you know a word. |
| Match-3, triple-match, Guess Who, detective | Match-3 and Tetris-likes are fun, but the **language is bolted on**: you can win by matching shapes without understanding a word, which fails our core test. Guess Who and detective games need lots of unique recorded dialogue per round, so they're costly and hard to replay. |
| Hidden object for everyone | Great for 7+, but a cluttered scene is too hard for a 5-year-old without a gentle mode. |

**The fix:**
- **Fewer modes**, each with its own **distinct core verb** and each passing both tests: *fun even if you don't care about learning*, and *can't be won without the Kutchi*.
- **Variety inside a mode** comes from *rule twists* (new ingredients, new stations, customer quirks), the way the hits do it, not from new engines.

## 3. How the hit games actually work, start to finish

### 3.1 *Good Pizza, Great Pizza* (restaurant, story-driven): **the closest fit for us**

1. **A day begins.** A counter, an empty pizza base and toppings in bins.
2. **A customer walks up and speaks an order.** Early on it's literal ("8 pepperoni"). Soon it becomes a **riddle**: "Pizza with *no red*", "Half cheese, half onion", "Surprise me, but I hate mushrooms", "The number of olives is my age minus 30". **Interpreting the request *is* the puzzle.**
3. **You build it with tactile verbs:** drag-spiral the sauce, sprinkle cheese by dragging, place toppings one by one (counting matters), then the oven, then slice (swipe lines).
4. **You serve it.** The customer reacts (happy, meh, angry) and **pays plus a tip based on accuracy**, not on speed. There's little or no timer; speed isn't the challenge, *understanding* is.
5. **Recurring characters** have personalities and small story arcs across days; you learn their tastes.
6. **End of day:** a money summary, then **buy upgrades and new toppings**. New toppings unlock new kinds of orders.
7. **Difficulty grows** through more complex riddles and more toppings, not through faster timers.

**Why it's fun:**
- The riddle "aha" moment.
- Tactile building.
- Characters you come to know.
- The day → upgrade → new possibilities loop.
- **No stress:** you think, then act.

**Fit for language:** perfect. Replace the English riddles with Kutchi requests. *Understanding the request is the gameplay.*

### 3.2 *Papa's Pizzeria / Freezeria* and *Cooking Fever* (restaurant, multitasking)

1. Customers queue with patience meters.
2. The order is shown as a **ticket** you can check at any time.
3. **Stations on separate screens:** order, build, bake or cook (a timing mini-game: stop in the green zone), finish (slice, pour, garnish).
4. You **juggle** several orders across stations: the multitasking is the fun.
5. **Each station is graded as a percentage** (placement accuracy, cook timing); combined, they set the tip.
6. **Rank-ups unlock** ingredients, customers and holidays (seasonal ingredient sets), plus a stickers and badges collection.

**Why it's fun:**
- A distinct mini-game verb per station.
- The juggling rush.
- Visible grading ("97%!").
- Constant unlocks.
- Seasonal variety.

**Fit for language:** good. The verbal instruction replaces the ticket. **But** juggling under a timer fights listening, so borrow the *stations and grading*, not the *heavy juggling*.

### 3.3 Hidden object (*June's Journey*, *Hidden City*)

1. A richly detailed static scene.
2. A list of 10–15 items (words, silhouettes or riddles).
3. Tap to find; **quick consecutive finds build a combo**; wrong taps cost a little time.
4. A hint that recharges.
5. **The same scene is replayed** with new item lists and **modifiers**: night mode (a torch circle), mirrored, silhouettes only, "find the differences". So content is cheap.
6. Scene mastery levels and story chapters unlock new scenes.

**Why it's fun:**
- Scanning, and the "found it!" moment.
- Beautiful scenes.
- Getting faster in a familiar scene.
- Modifiers keep the same scene fresh.

**Fit for language:** strong. The list is spoken Kutchi (and written for readers), with quantities and positions ("two onions on the top shelf"). Modifiers become language twists: "only what Nani *says*", "something *red*", "something *next to* the pot".

### 3.4 Match-3 (*Candy Crush*, *Royal Match*)

- **The loop:** swap two adjacent tiles; three in a row clear; the tiles above fall and **cascade**.
- **Specials:** four in a row makes a striped tile, five makes a colour bomb.
- **Each level** has a goal and a limited number of moves.
- **New obstacles** arrive every few levels.
- **Progress:** 1–3 stars, and a map of thousands of levels.

**Why it's fun:** a tiny verb, big chain reactions (partly luck), near misses, and clearly defined goals.

**Fit for language:** **poor.** You never need a word to play. At most, meta (Nani's spoken goals). **Skip.**

### 3.5 Block puzzles (*Block Blast*, Tetris)

- **The loop:** place pieces on a grid; clear full lines; there's no timer and it runs endlessly for a high score.
- **Why it's fun:** spatial satisfaction and flow.
- **Fit for language:** **poor. Skip.**

### 3.6 Simon / sequence memory

- **The loop:** watch or hear a sequence, repeat it, and the sequence grows by one each round.
- **Why it's fun:** tension, the "got it!" moment, and trying to beat your personal best.
- **Fit for language:** **strong** when the sequence is spoken instructions ("wash, cut, add salt, stir"). It works best as **a stage inside cooking**, not as a game of its own. Zafar's instinct ("you're told or shown, then recreate it") is exactly this.

### 3.7 Tycoon (*Lemonade Stand*, *Zoo Tycoon*, *Cafeland*)

- **The loop:** decide prices, stock and upgrades, then watch customers respond and money grow.
- **Why it's fun:** optimising, and watching a place get better.
- **Fit for language:** weak as the *core*, excellent as the **meta layer**. Coins from the mini-games buy upgrades for Nani's kitchen, stall and house (new recipes, a bigger stall, décor). That's what keeps Zafar-type and Maryam-type players coming back.

### 3.8 Rhythm (*Magic Tiles*)

- **The loop:** tap in time with music.
- **Why it's fun:** music and flow.
- **Fit for language:** medium: call-and-response counting songs and a dhol. Great for 4–6 year olds as a **toy interlude**, but it costs music production. **Later.**

### 3.9 Daily word puzzle (*Wordle*)

- **The loop:** one puzzle a day, a shareable result, a streak.
- **Why it's fun:** a tiny commitment and a social ritual.
- **Fit for language:** strong for adults and readers ("Kutchi word of the day", or a listening challenge). **Cheap.** The Farah persona.

## 4. What actually makes games fun (the checklist every mode must pass)

1. **A core verb that feels good the 500th time** (dragging sauce, swiping to chop, tapping a found object).
2. **A clear goal in each short round** (1–3 minutes) and visible progress within it.
3. **Mastery:** you can get better, and the game shows it (grades, combos, personal bests).
4. **Variety through rule twists,** not new games: new ingredient, new customer quirk, new modifier.
5. **Juice:** instant feedback (bounce, pop, sound, particles, a customer's reaction).
6. **A meta loop:** coins → upgrades and unlocks → new possibilities in the next round.
7. **Characters you care about** (recurring family members with tastes and personalities).
8. **Surprise:** randomised orders and occasional special events (Eid rush, a guest with an odd request).
9. **Tension then release,** but never punishment for the language itself.
10. **The language is the input:** you can't win without understanding the Kutchi.

## 5. Revised lineup: 3 core games plus a meta layer, each with its own verb

| Place | Game | Core verb | Built on | Why it's different from the others |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nani's kitchen** | **Cook with Nani** | *Build* (wash, chop, stir, pour, roll, time the cook) | *Good Pizza, Great Pizza* + Papa's stations + Simon sequences | Tactile cooking; riddle orders; recipes learned as sequences |
| **The bazaar** | **Bazaar hunt** | *Search* (scan a busy scene, tap to find) + count out coins | Hidden object | Scanning, combos, and a scene that changes with modifiers |
| **The house** | **Get the house ready** | *Arrange* (place things where told; lay the dastarkhwan; hang decorations) | *Unpacking* / sorting satisfaction | Calm, satisfying order-making; positions and colours |
| **Everywhere** | **Nani's house (meta)** | *Decorate and upgrade* | Tycoon-lite, *Animal Crossing* home | Coins from every game; the place visibly grows |
| **Daily** | **Nani's word of the day** | Listen and pick | *Wordle* | A 1-minute daily habit and streak |

**Dropped:** stall rush (it duplicated the kitchen), match-3 and block puzzles (the language isn't needed), Guess Who and detective (content-heavy, low replay), and rhythm (later, as a toy for little ones).

### Cook with Nani, in detail (the flagship)

**A "service" (one round, about 3 minutes):**
1. **A family member comes in and asks in Kutchi.** Early orders are literal: "Two rotis and daal." Later they're **riddles**:
   - "Nana's chai, the way he likes it" (you remember: no sugar);
   - "Something sweet, but not mango";
   - "Enough rotis for everyone at the table" (count the people).
2. **Collect ingredients** from the pantry shelves by name (listening).
3. **Prepare at stations,** each a small tactile verb: *scrub* to wash (rub), *chop* (swipe the number of times Nani says), *roll* roti (drag outwards), *stir* (circle), *pour* (hold and release at the line), *cook* (tap to stop in the green zone).
4. **Recipe sequences (Simon):** the first time, Nani demonstrates each step and says it. In later services she only *says* the steps, and you recreate the order. Mastered recipes can be cooked from memory for a bonus.
5. **Serve.** A character reaction, then a **grade per step** plus a tip. A total for the day, and **stars**.
6. **Between services:** spend coins on new recipes (chai → roti → daal → khichdi → Eid sweets), utensils and kitchen upgrades. New recipes bring new verbs and new words.

**Settings:**
- **Relaxed:** no timer; customers wait forever. Suits 4–6 year olds.
- **Busy:** gentle patience meters; more time for new words.

**Variety without new engines:**
- **Customers:** each family member has fixed tastes.
- **Events:** the Eid rush, guests arriving, a power cut (cooking by lantern light).
- **Recipes:** each new recipe changes which stations you use.

**Language it trains:** food, quantities, cooking verbs, sequence words (first, then), preferences (likes, doesn't like), kinship, comparisons (more, less, sweeter).

## 6. Answering "won't it be repetitive?"

- **Across modes:** yes, if the core verb is shared. The revised lineup has **three different verbs**: build, search, arrange. Shared plumbing (audio, scoring, coins, word progress) stays invisible.
- **Within a mode:** repetition is fixed the way the hits fix it:
  - a steady drip of **new rule twists** (a new recipe or station every day or two);
  - **characters** with tastes;
  - **events**;
  - **mastery goals** (3 stars, from memory, a perfect grade).
- **The story** then sends you to each place for a reason, and the meta layer rewards playing all three.

## 7. Recommendation: what to build first

**Build a rough (greybox) prototype of *Cook with Nani*:** one kitchen, one full service loop, playable end to end.
- **Customers:** 3 family members, each with a spoken order (placeholder audio or text until the family records).
- **Recipes:** chai (pour, stir, sugar count), fruit plate (collect, chop by count, arrange) and roti (roll, cook timing).
- **Stations:** pantry pick, chop, pour, stir, cook: each one tactile and juicy.
- **Sequences:** Nani demonstrates the chai steps first, then asks you to repeat them.
- **Scoring:** grade per step, tips, coins, 1–3 stars, and an end-of-day summary with one upgrade to buy.
- **Settings:** relaxed and busy.
- **Art:** plain shapes and existing art only. **The point is to find out whether it's fun**, not to make it pretty.
- **Playtest with the family:** do the kids ask for another service? Does Zafar?

If it passes, it becomes the template for the Bazaar hunt and the house game, and the art direction (being decided in parallel) is applied on top.
