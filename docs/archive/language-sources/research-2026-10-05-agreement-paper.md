## 1. In plain English

The verb's ending changes to match someone, usually the **doer**: he or she, one or more, and I / you / they. The paper calls this **complete** agreement. It's used in the present, the future, and the past without an object ("Mohan came").

In the past **with an object** ("read the book", "made the chai"), agreement is **defective**:

- **Doer "you", "he" or "she":** the verb shows only one or more. No gender; a single doer gets the ending *-e*.
- **Doer "I":** the verb matches the **thing** instead. With "we", either.
- **With the "went" helper** (like *khai vyo*), the verb matches the doer in full again (pp. 44–45).

Respelled into our spelling, with the paper's form in brackets:

| # | Sentence | Word by word | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Mohan aavyo · Rina aavai* (a:v-yo, a:v-ai) | Mohan came-he · Rina came-she | Mohan came. Rina came. |
| 2 | *Ram chopri waanche we · Rina chopri waanche we* (vã:ts.-e ve) | Ram/Rina book read-*e* was-*e* | Ram / Rina had read the book: the same ending for both. |
| 3 | *Aau hu chaapo waanchyo* (vã:ts.-yo) | I that newspaper read-he | I read that newspaper (man or woman). |
| 4 | *Aau hu chopri waanchai* (vã:ts.-ai) | I that book read-she | I read that book (man or woman). |
| 5 | *Aau chopriyu waanche vaiya / vyo* (vaiyã: / vyo) | I books read went-I-she / went-he | I read the books (a woman / a man). |
| 6 | *Hu chokri hi gaayno gaaindhi wi* (ga:-indh-i: vi:) | that girl this song sing-ing-she was-she | That girl used to sing this song. |

## 2. Which Kutchi the paper describes

- **Where and who:** Kutch district, Gujarat. The paper names three varieties: Abhdasa (west and north), Kanthi (centre and south) and Waghad (east). It "mainly" describes **Kanthi Kutchi as spoken by the Oswal community** (p. 4, footnote 1). The second author is listed as an independent scholar; the paper refers to "native speakers" (p. 15) but doesn't say how many people were asked.
- **Is it the family's language, or a Gujarati variety?** It's **Kutchi proper, the Sindhi-related language**, not "Kutchi Gujarati". The paper says so directly: Kutchi "is distinct from Kutchi Gujarati", it "shares features with Sindhi", and Kutchi Gujarati shares features with Marwari (p. 4, footnote 1; §5.2, pp. 40–43, where the two are compared). So the caution in `README.md` ("may be a Gujarati variety") applies to the *other* paper (Patel-Grosz and Grosz), not this one.
- **But it's a different variety and community from the family's.** Many of its words match the family's (*aau, tu, toke, anke, hi, ai, ain, aiya, aiye, aayo, chokra, nar*), but some core words differ (§3 below): "come" is *aav* (the family says *ach*), "he/she" is *hu* (the family says *e*), "tomorrow" is *kaal* (Mum: *saware*). So the paper is a map of how Kutchi *can* work, not a record of how the family speaks.
- **Script and spelling:** no Gujarati script. Romanised in a linguist's phonetic spelling: a colon for a long vowel (*a:*), a tilde for a nasal vowel (*ũ*), *ts.* for the sound we write *ch* (*ts.okro* = *chokro*), *n.* and *r.* for retroflex sounds (p. 7, footnote 6). The respellings in this file convert *a:* → *aa*, *ts.* → *ch*, *ε* → *e*, a word-initial *v* → *w* (rule G4), and drop the nasal marks, which our spelling doesn't show.

**Mum's *nares* (§20):** in the paper's system, "I saw Simba" should match Simba (*naryo*, or a default *-yo* because Simba has *ke*: p. 13, footnote 7). Mum's *nares* doesn't fit. It might be *nare* + an "I" ending, which the paper's variety doesn't have. It's worth a yes/no question, not a conclusion.

## 4. What to take, learn or change

Each is marked **adopt now**, **test with Mum first** or **ignore**.

1. **Widen the engine's one past-tense setting into a small table. Adopt now** (design only; every value stays `unknown`). `engine-design.md` §4 has a single `pastTransitiveAgreesWith` setting. The paper shows the answer can depend on **who** the doer is: "I" → the thing; "we" → either; "you / he / she" → the doer's one-or-more only. Key the setting by the doer's person and number, in data. `engine-design.md` risk 2 expected this. **We're on the path.**
2. **Reserve a "no gender" verb form, the *-e* ending. Adopt now** as an empty `hyp` slot. If Mum confirms it, a past-with-object verb needs one more form per verb (*kape*, "cut", for any one doer). Reserving it costs nothing and avoids a data-format change later.
3. **Prefer the family's "went" compounds for the game's past lines. Test with Mum first.** The family already says *khai vyo*, *thai vai*, *achi vya*. In the paper, the "went" helper brings back ordinary agreement with the doer. If Mum agrees, lines like "Who ate the sweets?", "The chai's gone cold" and "The maani burned!" need no split at all. Doubt: *banai vai* may sound wrong for "made". The yes/no list checks it.
4. **"I made it" may need no boy and girl versions. Test with Mum first.** If the verb matches the thing, *Aau bateto kapyo* ("I cut the potato") is the same for a boy and a girl, unlike "I will" (*kar dis / dos*, §32). That halves the child's past-tense recordings. Round 5 L74, L80, C128 and C129 settle it: listen for whether Mum's man and woman versions come out the same.
5. **Noun gender matters even more. Adopt now** (it's already the plan). If past verbs follow the thing, every Cook noun's gender changes the verb. Keep the gender questions (Round 5 L34–L50) at the top. **Already on the path.**
6. **"used to" and "was" are cheap to ask. Test with Mum first** (yes/no list, Part 4). Story mode will need "Nani used to make maani" and "Where was the spoon?" (*kida wi?*).
7. **Keep *kere* (who, as the doer) on the list. Test with Mum first.** The paper shows that a special doer form for "who" or "he" is real in at least one Kutchi variety (Abhdasa, p. 40). That makes Mum's *kere karein?* more believable. Don't drop Round 5 Q10.
8. **Read *khapeto / khapeti* as stem + a *to / ti* helper. Test with Mum first** (Round 5 C110–C114). If it's the paper's "-ing" helper, one rule covers *bareto*, *ukreto* and *khapeti*, and the engine gets the whole present tense from it.
9. **Update `grammar-kb.md` feature 16 and the README. Adopt now** (docs only; the orchestrator's call, not done here). Feature 16 says "abstract only (the PDF is blocked)": it can now cite this file. The README's "matches" list says *aayo* = "came"; for the family, *aayo* is "you are" to an elder (§21, §51).
10. **Ignore the theory** (probes, feature geometry, impoverishment, pp. 18–39). It explains *why* the pattern exists, but nothing in it changes what the game says.
11. **Ignore the paper's words** (*aav* "come", *waanch* "read", *chopri* "book", *kaal* "tomorrow", *hu* "he"). They're a different community's words. Mum's win.
12. **Ignore "we" for now.** With "we" the paper allows either agreement, with a difference in emphasis (p. 15). The game rarely says "we made it". Revisit only if a line needs it.

**The bottom line:** we already knew the split might exist (feature 16), and the engine already keeps it in data. The paper adds three things: the answer depends on *who* the doer is; the "went" helper switches the split off; and a gender-free *-e* form may exist. Round 5 C123–C136 and L80–L82, plus the yes/no list, will tell us which of these the family does.
