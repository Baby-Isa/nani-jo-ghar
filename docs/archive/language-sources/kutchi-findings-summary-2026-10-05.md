# What we found in a Kutchi book and a computer's Kutchi stories

*5 October 2026. A short read for Mum (and anyone who's curious).*

Zafar found two things to compare with your answers. One is a paper by three language researchers who studied how Kutchi verbs work. The other is a set of little stories and conversations that a computer program, Gemini, wrote "in Kutchi". We read both closely and compared them with everything you've recorded for us.

**The short version: you're the expert, not them.** Neither source is how our family speaks. But they're full of interesting clues, and they've helped us write a short list of yes / no questions for you.

## 1. The researchers' Kutchi is a cousin of ours

The researchers asked Kutchi speakers from one community in central Kutch. So much of what they wrote down matches you exactly:

- *Aau rasore me **aiya**. Tu rasore me **aiye**. Aai rasore me **aayo**.* (I'm / you're / you're, to an elder, in the kitchen.) Their "am, are, is" words are yours, and they also found that *aiya* is the same for a man and a woman, just as you said.
- *aai* for an elder takes the "more than one" ending, just as you explained.
- The two "we"s: their *asi* and *paan* are your ***asa*** (without you) and ***pa*** (with you).
- *toke*, *anke*, *chokro → chokra*, *chokriyu*, and *nar* for "look": all yours.

And some things are different, which shows it's a neighbouring way of speaking:

- They say *aav* for "come"; you say ***ach*** (*hida ach*).
- They use *hu* for "he"; you say ***e***, and keep *hu* for "that".
- Their "tomorrow" is *kaal*. You told us *kale* sounds Gujarati and the family says ***saware***. Your instinct matches what we'd expect: their variety leans a little more towards Gujarati.

## 2. The most interesting thing: "I ate it" works differently

In most sentences the verb follows the person doing it. You showed us this beautifully with *kar dis* (a girl says it) and *kar dos* (a boy).

The researchers found something surprising about the **past**, when there's a thing involved ("I cut the potato", "I read the book"). In their Kutchi, when *I* did it, the verb follows the **thing**, not the person:

- *Aau bateto kapyo.* "I cut the potato": *-yo* because the potato is a he-word, even if a girl says it.
- *Aau dungri kapi.* "I cut the onion": *-i* because the onion is a she-word.

When *Nani* or *Nana* did it, they found yet another ending, *-e*, the same for both (*Nani dungri kape*).

Here's the lovely part: **your way of saying the past already avoids all this.** You say ***Simba khai vyo***, ***film khalas thai vai***, ***ker mitai khai vyo?***, and the researchers found that the little "went" word (*vyo / vai*) brings the verb back to following the person. So your everyday Kutchi may be the simpler pattern. We just need to check how you'd say a few sentences.

## 3. The computer's stories: some good, some made up

Gemini wrote a story about a farmer, some market chats, two cooks making rotlo and kadhi, and a host welcoming a guest. Some of it sounds right:

- ***Acho*** / ***ach*** for "come", ***kha***, ***de***, ***kar / karo***, ***hal***: your words exactly.
- ***khani aach*** for "bring": just like your ***khanech***.
- ***khalas***, ***wadhare***, ***jaldi***, ***barabar***, ***garam***, ***pela … poi***: all yours.
- *paki vyo* (it's ripened) and *wiki vai* (it's sold out) use the same "went" word as your *thai vyo / thai vai*.

But plenty of it is Gujarati, Hindi or invented:

- It says *be* for two. You say ***ba***.
- It writes "I" three different ways (*Ma*, *Aao*, *Moo*). You say ***aau***.
- It says *kiya* for "where". You say ***kida***.
- It uses *mat* for "don't" (that's Hindi). You put ***na*** after the verb.
- It slipped Hindi script into the middle of a Kutchi sentence twice, and some parts are full of long formal words no Nani would say to a five-year-old.

A few of its words might be real and useful, and only you can say: ***pi*** (drink), ***chhonk*** (tadka), ***limdo*** (curry leaves), ***hing***, ***mathho*** (buttermilk), ***chinta na kar*** (don't worry), ***aaraam kar*** (have a rest), and for the doctor's game ***lohi*** (blood) and ***haddo*** (bone).
