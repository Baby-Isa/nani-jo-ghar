## Part 1: Grammar patterns

Needed before the word list gets built into the game, so the spreadsheet has the right columns from the start rather than getting redone later.

### 1. He-words and she-words

In Kutchi, is every object either a "he" word or a "she" word, the way it works in Hindi, Gujarati and Sindhi? If so:

- Does a describing word change depending on which one it is? For example, would "big" said about a house sound different to "big" said about a table, if house and table are different genders?
- A few examples to say out loud, whatever comes to mind: a cup, a door, a chair, a mango, a shirt. For each, is it a "he" word or a "she" word?

### 2. Plurals

For two or three everyday objects (a cup, a mango, a shirt are fine again): how do you say "two cups", "two mangoes", "two shirts"? Does the word for the object itself change, or does only the number in front of it change?

### 3. Six position phrases

Please say each of these naturally, with any object you like standing in for "it": under, behind, next to, in front of, between (two things), on top of.

For example: "the cat is under the table", "the key is behind the door". However you'd actually say it at home is exactly what's wanted, not a formal or textbook version.

### 4. Three sentences, past tense

Please say these three naturally, as you'd actually say them to a grandchild:

1. "I went to the market" (nobody else involved)
2. "She gave me the mangoes" (someone gave something to someone)
3. "It stopped raining" (something just happened, on its own)

These three are chosen because early evidence suggests the first kind (nobody else involved) and the second and third kind (someone did something to something) may work differently in Kutchi's past tense, so hearing all three said naturally settles it.

### 5. Five phrases still needed for the bazaar errand

These were flagged as outstanding when the first errand was written. Any of Claude's guesses at these must not be used; only your own wording ships.

1. "What would you like?"
2. "How many?"
3. "Well done!"
4. "I've already put in the [papaya and kiwi]…" (Nani naming things already added to the bowl)
5. "Give me the…" / "Add the…"

### 6. Checking the existing fixed frame

The game currently uses this frame for every request, with one word changing in the middle: **"Muke {word} khape"**, meant as "I need {word}".

- Is that how you'd naturally say "I need the lemons" or "I need two oranges"?
- Does anything in that sentence change if you're asking for more than one of something, or if the thing you need is a "he" word versus a "she" word?

If it's wrong or unnatural, the game's fixed frame changes to whatever you'd actually say; that's more important than keeping this exact wording.

## Part 2: A first pass at the word list, grounded in real family life

Rather than a bare list of "fruit words, vegetable words, clothes words", each section below is anchored in something real: a dish you actually cook, an Eid morning, the people in the family. The words fall out of the real thing, and that's also what will make the game's food, clothes and kinship errands feel like this specific family rather than a generic one.

### 7. Five family dishes

Name five dishes that are genuinely part of our home cooking, not a generic idea of Kutchi Memon or Khoja food. For each one:

- The dish's name
- 5 to 10 ingredients that go into it: fruit, vegetables, spices, staples, meat, whatever it actually needs
- If it belongs to a particular occasion (Eid morning, everyday dinner, a guest's visit, a rainy day), say so, since that helps tie it into the story

This is the main source for the game's fruit, vegetable, spice and staple word lists, so real dishes matter more than a complete pantry inventory.

### 8. Household requests

Walk around the kitchen and the house, in your head or out loud, and name half a dozen things you'd actually ask a grandchild to fetch, put away, or wash: a bowl from a shelf, a spoon from a drawer, a mat to lay out, shoes to line up. Real requests, not a room-by-room inventory.

### 9. Eid morning

- What does everyone wear that morning? Name each item (a kurta, a headscarf, a prayer cap, sandals, a shawl).
- How would you greet an elder that morning, in Kutchi?
- What's the phrase for giving or asking for Eidi (the Eid pocket money)?

### 10. Family and kinship titles

Which of these does our side of the family actually use, and what do you call each one:

- Father's brother, and his wife
- Mother's brother
- Mother's sister
- Father's sister
- An older girl cousin
- What would a grandchild call you, and what would they call [Dad's father / your husband]?

This maps directly onto the game's family cast (Nana, Masi, Mama, Kaka, Kaki, Fui, older cousin), so it's worth getting our own family's actual terms rather than the textbook set.

### 11. Straight dictation

No recipe or scene needed for these, just say the list, whatever comes to mind:

- **Colours:** red, blue, green, yellow, orange, purple, pink, black, white, brown
- **Weather:** sun, rain, cloud, wind, hot, cold, it's cloudy, it's raining
- **Numbers 1 to 10**
- **Greetings and courtesy:** hello / peace be upon you, how are you, thank you, please, goodbye, well done, the Eid greeting
