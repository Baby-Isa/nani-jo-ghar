# Quick yes / no list for Mum (5 Oct 2026)

**How to answer.** Read each line out loud. Then say one of:

- **"Yes"**, if you'd say it just like that;
- **"No"**, if you wouldn't;
- **"I'd say …"**, and then say it your way. That's the most useful answer of all.

There are no wrong answers. Many of these lines come from a book about Kutchi or from a computer, so plenty of them **should** get a "no". Each line takes a few seconds. It's fine to stop at any point; the lines at the top matter most.

*For the grown-ups:* the lines come from `docs/language/sources/research-2026-10-05-agreement-paper.md` (the paper's patterns, respelled) and `docs/language/sources/gemini-excerpts-2026-10-05.md` (Gemini's words). They are **guesses to test, not Kutchi**: nothing here goes into the game unless Mum says it (rule G1). Record the session in one take, saying each number, as usual (rule G16). Spellings are ours (rule G4).

*For the grown-ups (what each part tests):*

- **Part 1:** whether the past-with-an-object verb matches the doer, the thing, or neither (the paper's *-e*: items 1, 7, 8). Whether a girl and a boy say "I cut it" the same way (10–11). Whether the *vyo / vai* helper works with "cut" (9). Whether *kere* is the "who" for a doer (14–15). Whether agreement carries into "has boiled / burnt / gone cold" (16–19).
- **Part 4:** the *wo / wi / wa* "was" and the "used to" form the paper predicts; Mum's own *nares* (51–52); "we" (53–54); the old *me* for "I" (55).
- **Parts 2, 3 and 5:** words from Gemini's excerpts, which may well be Gujarati or made up. The family's words always win.
